about the project

Open weights is not open source.

You wouldn’t call a compiled binary “open source” because you can download it. A model without its training data is exactly that — because the training data is the model’s source code. OpenWALDO exists to open it: a shared, collaborative foundation that anyone can obtain, inspect, and contribute to.

the case

Why open source AI — and why now.

The industry argued itself into a corner

The labs selling closed access spent years insisting that openly released models are dangerous — a story as old as GPT-2, withheld in 2019 as too unsafe to release in full, though the predicted harms never arrived. The same labs asked Congress to license who may build powerful models and signed statements ranking their own products alongside pandemics and nuclear war. It is an argument their business depends on: a model you can only rent through an API is a moat. Then labs in China began releasing open-weight models that match — and sometimes beat — the closed frontier, freely and by the dozen. The scarcity story collapsed, and the bans proposed in response are nearly unenforceable against weights anyone can download.

Both sides are afraid of the same thing

All of it gets the threat model half right. The problem was never where a model comes from. The problem is that nobody can audit what went into it. The fear of closed Western models and the fear of open Chinese ones are the same fear — being forced to trust weights no one can see into.

What’s missing isn’t policy. It’s an auditable source of data, and the toolchain to build trusted models from it.

Open source has won this argument before

Proprietary vendors made identical claims about open code — insecure, unaccountable, impossible to trust — and they lost, because source, licenses, and provenance made trust unnecessary: anyone could read the code, and anyone could rebuild it. Linux didn’t win by being certified safe; it won by being inspectable, forkable, and community-owned. AI is missing exactly that property, and no export rule, ban, or safety pledge can substitute for it.

Closed data is the root of the fights we’re having

Look at what people are actually angry about in AI right now. Authors and artists finding their work in training sets they never agreed to. Labs accusing each other of distillation. Companies unable to see inside the models they depend on. Courts untangling what was pirated and what was licensed. Every one of these conflicts is a symptom of the same disease: the data is closed, so nobody can prove anything — not consent, not license, not origin, not absence. Make the data genuinely open source — licensed, attributed, removable, on the record — and those problems don’t get argued away. They disappear, the same way open source dissolved the argument about trusting code.

A copy of a copy of a copy

Three things go into every AI model: smart people, lots of compute, and training data. And all training data is distilled from other sources — literally all of it. So is it wrong to distill training data from training data that was already distilled from someone else? The industry can’t answer, because nobody can see the chain. And the deeper problem isn’t even the ethics — it’s the engineering: a copy of a copy of a copy gets worse and less specific with every generation, until the training data itself starts to hallucinate — the degradation researchers call model collapse. A game of telephone, played at a trillion tokens.

OpenWALDO skips the telephone line. It brings the sources together in a distributable format that anyone can trust, audit, and leverage directly — no distilling from other models, no inheriting someone else’s copy. Get it from the source.

A commons, not a product

No single company can build this, and none should own it. What AI needs is what every layer of trusted infrastructure before it needed: a shared, collaborative base foundation — a corpus of knowledge that anyone can obtain and anyone can contribute to — with licenses, provenance, and the right to be removed built into the format itself. OpenWALDO is that foundation: a cryptographically attributed corpus, meaning every shard is verifiable by hash, every contribution is attested by a named person, and every license and origin is on the record. A model built on it carries its source: every training run records a bill of materials, so anyone can audit it, verify it, or recreate it. The corpus is the commons; the models are proof it works.

And the name? For years, everyone has been asking the same question about training data — where is it? Where did it come from? Who said you could use it? This project exists so the answer can finally, cheerfully, be: “Here’s WALDO.” Stripes and all.
how the project runs

In the open, like everything worth trusting.

Everything happens in git

The corpus is curated by pull request; every contribution carries a DCO sign-off under the contributor’s own name. There is no side channel — the repository history is the project’s history.

Peer review is the gate

The license bar is enforced by human review, the way every serious open source project keeps itself honest — license claims checked, sources verified, provenance recorded.

Nobody owns the bytes

Training data is hosted by contributors themselves on federated, content-addressed storage. The project holds the map, not the territory — no single point of control, or failure.

Funding-honest, laptop-first

The roadmap says plainly what each stage costs: today’s rungs train on laptops and single GPUs; the foundation model will take partners. No vaporware, no pretending compute is free.

not an AI lab

We don’t build the models. We build what models are built on.

OpenWALDO’s goal is not to be an AI lab. The project doesn’t train frontier models, doesn’t compete with the people who do, and has no ambition to start. What it builds is the substrate: the licensed, provenance-tracked corpus and the toolchain that turns it into auditable models — for the AI labs, companies, researchers, and communities who do the training. At most, the project will publish small reference models to prove the pipeline works end to end — think of them as the corpus’s test suite, not a product.

the birth of openwaldo

Founded by Gregory M. Kurtzer.

LinkedIn X/Twitter

Greg has spent his career building open source projects and the communities that carry them: co-founder of CentOS, creator of Warewulf and of Singularity (now the Linux Foundation’s Apptainer), and founder of Rocky Linux and the Rocky Enterprise Software Foundation — communities counted in the tens of millions, built on the promise that essential infrastructure stays free. That instinct comes from nearly two decades in high-performance computing at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, where the software that mattered was open and the science that mattered was shared.

The pattern across all of it: find the place where openness is required, build the foundation, and grow the community that makes it permanent. As a huge advocate of AI, Greg sees that place clearly — the data. So he’s doing what he has always done: creating the open, shared, collaborative foundation, and inviting everyone to join. The invitation is real, and it’s open.

“There are brilliant AI minds everywhere — far better than mine. My goal, and my passion, is to give people a way to work together. I’ve spent my career building communities that end up bigger than any founder, and that’s exactly the plan: a substrate where we all come together and collaborate. If you know models, data, community, law, language, or infrastructure, join the community and let's do something amazing!” — Greg
