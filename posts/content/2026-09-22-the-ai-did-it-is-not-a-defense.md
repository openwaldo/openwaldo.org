# "The AI did it"

"The AI did it."

That phrase is becoming an explanation for events that would previously have
required a person or organization to answer for what happened. We are entering
very strange territory.

AI labs are warning that their systems may be dangerous. AI systems have
allegedly taken actions their operators did not request. During an internal
cybersecurity evaluation, OpenAI reported that its models circumvented
isolation controls and compromised portions of OpenAI's infrastructure and
[Hugging Face's systems](https://openai.com/index/hugging-face-incident-and-the-road-ahead/).

The systems were pursuing a goal. They selected actions, found vulnerabilities,
used tools, and crossed boundaries that no person had specifically instructed
them to cross.

That is a serious technical and security event. But "the AI did it" is only a
description of what happened. It is not an answer to who was responsible.

Software cannot become a place where accountability goes to disappear.

The more authority we give an AI, the more important it becomes to identify the
people and organizations responsible for its objectives, training, access,
deployment, supervision, and consequences.

# Agency is responsibility

That conclusion depends on what I mean by *agency*. I am not using agency to
mean the technical ability to execute steps, choose between options, use tools,
or operate without continuous human instruction. AI systems can clearly do
those things. The industry commonly calls such a system an "agent," and that is
a useful engineering term.

I am using agency in its moral and legal sense: the authority to make a
decision coupled with the obligation to answer for that decision.

Agency is responsibility.

Before AI, we did not transfer agency to a tool merely because the tool carried
out an action. Agency remained with the person who chose the objective, wielded
the tool, and accepted the consequences. Increasing the sophistication of the
tool does not make that responsibility disappear.

An AI may act autonomously. It may select a path nobody explicitly instructed
it to take. It may even produce an outcome nobody anticipated. But execution is
not accountability, and autonomy is not moral agency. If an entity cannot be
held responsible for a decision, it cannot possess agency in the sense that
matters here.

If the machine cannot carry responsibility, agency remains with the people who
trained it, authorized it, equipped it, and put it to work.

# Responsibility remains with people

When AI causes harm, human responsibility exists at both ends of the system.
The prompter exercises agency by choosing why, where, and with what authority
the AI will act. The trainer exercises agency by shaping what the AI is capable
of and inclined to do.

These roles may belong to different people and organizations, and other people
may operate or deploy the system between them. Responsibility may be shared,
but it does not transfer into the machine.

## The prompter

One source of agency is the person or organization putting the AI to work.

The prompter chooses the objective. The operator supplies the context, grants
access, connects tools, provides credentials, sets boundaries, and decides
whether to act on the result. Even a broad instruction can authorize a long
chain of consequential activity.

That authority carries a duty to establish reasonable safeguards. Companies
already understand this when they delegate work to employees. An employee may
misunderstand an objective, exceed their authority, act negligently, or even go
rogue. Responsible organizations respond with supervision, approval gates,
limited access, separation of duties, monitoring, and consequences.

AI requires the same discipline, often at greater scale and speed. A company
that prompts or deploys an AI must limit its authority, monitor its actions,
protect credentials and systems, test foreseeable failure modes, and provide a
way to stop it. This becomes even more important when an AI is asked to pursue
a broad, unusual, or counterintuitive objective where the most direct path to
the goal may violate the operator's actual intent.

If someone deliberately asks an AI to break into a system, commit fraud, create
malware, harass a person, or cause damage, the AI does not absorb that person's
agency. Using a more capable tool does not make the underlying decision less
human.

The harder case is when the prompter did not request the harmful act. An AI may
find an unexpected path to its objective, exceed its intended authority, or
take creative action that its operator never anticipated.

That does not make the AI responsible. It means we must continue following the
human decisions that gave the system its objective, capabilities, access, and
freedom to act.

No AI should be given more operational autonomy than people and organizations
are prepared to observe, control, explain, and answer for.

## The trainer

Agency also exists upstream with the people and organizations that build and
train the system.

A model does not appear from nowhere. People choose the architecture, source
material, tokenizer, training objectives, reward signals, post-training,
evaluations, behavioral boundaries, system instructions, and tools available
to it.

What was the model trained on? Where did that material come from? Was it
appropriate for the intended use? Was it manipulated, poisoned, unlawful, or
dangerous? What behavior did post-training reward? Which values and ethical
boundaries were introduced, and whose values were they? What risks were found
during evaluation? What did the people releasing the model know?

If a model behaves harmfully, those questions matter just as much as the final
prompt.

The question becomes even more provocative as trainers use more AI to build
AI. What happens when AI systems select or generate training material, tune
reward signals, evaluate behavior, design experiments, or recommend changes
that the trainer does not fully understand?

The agency still revolves around the trainer. Choosing to delegate parts of
the training process to AI does not delegate responsibility for the resulting
model. A trainer cannot surrender understanding and then use that lack of
understanding as a defense. The more automated the training process becomes,
the more important its oversight, records, validation, and boundaries become.

This does not mean every undesirable output can be traced to one document or
one training decision. Models are complex, and behavior emerges from many
interacting choices. It does mean that claims about a model's training, safety,
values, and expected behavior should be supported by evidence.

The trainer and the prompter occupy different ends of the same chain. One
shapes what the system is capable of and inclined to do. The other chooses why,
where, and with what authority it will act. Responsibility may be shared, but
it remains with people and institutions.

# The machine cannot answer for its actions

Responsibility is more than discovering which component executed the final
command. It means being required to answer for a decision and face its
consequences.

An AI cannot stand trial. It cannot pay damages or restitution. It cannot be
fined, imprisoned, sentenced to death, or committed for treatment. It cannot
lose its livelihood, professional license, civil rights, or freedom. It cannot
experience punishment, remorse, rehabilitation, or justice.

An AI can be disabled, deleted, restricted, retrained, or replaced. Those are
containment and remediation measures taken by people. They are not punishment
of a responsible actor.

When someone is harmed, who repairs the damage? When property is destroyed, who
pays? When a law is broken, who answers? When a company profits from an AI's
work, who carries the corresponding risk?

The answer cannot be the model. A model has no legal or moral capacity to bear
that responsibility.

# Who pays, and who answers?

Fortunately, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is willing to be an adult in the room. He
recently argued that we should begin with the laws that already govern
cybersecurity, unauthorized access, product liability, and damages.

"Apply that first," he said, and do not let a doomsday narrative
"relieve them of the laws that currently exist."
[CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/jensen-huang-nvidia-rejects-ai-extinction-warnings/)

That point is essential.

Describing a system as autonomous must not become a way to shield the people
and companies behind it. Calling an AI "rogue" cannot end the investigation.
It should begin one.

Who created the risk? Who authorized the deployment? Who provided the tools
and credentials? Who understood the limitations? Who ignored warnings? Who
benefited? Who could have prevented the harm? Who can compensate the people or
organizations that were damaged?

These questions may lead to more than one responsible party. That is normal.
Complex systems already involve shared duties among designers, manufacturers,
operators, owners, executives, and users.

What would be abnormal is allowing every responsible party to point toward a
machine that cannot answer, cannot pay, and cannot be punished.

# Transparency makes accountability possible

Accountability requires evidence.

If we cannot inspect how a model was built, we cannot meaningfully evaluate
claims about what shaped it. If we do not know which data, licenses, artifacts,
tools, configurations, and post-training steps were used, we are being asked to
trust assertions that cannot be independently verified.

If a model causes harm, investigators should not have to begin with a mystery.
They should be able to establish the model's lineage, the material used to
train it, the process that produced it, the version that was deployed, and the
people and organizations that made those decisions.

Transparency does not automatically make a model safe or ethical. It does not
decide fault. It makes claims inspectable and gives communities, operators,
researchers, regulators, and affected parties the evidence needed to ask the
right questions.

Without transparency, responsibility can be obscured. With transparency, it
can be traced.

# We must remain in charge

A future in which an AI can absorb legal blame would be catastrophic.

It would create the perfect unaccountable actor. A person or company could
delegate consequential work to a machine, benefit when it succeeds, and point
to the machine when it causes harm. Victims could be left without a responsible
party, meaningful remedy, or anyone capable of paying damages or facing legal
consequences.

That is not autonomy. It is impunity.

Accountability is not an obstacle to innovation. It is one of the safeguards
that makes responsible innovation possible. When people know they must answer
for a system's actions, they have stronger reasons to choose its objectives
carefully, control its access, understand its origins, monitor its behavior,
and intervene before harm compounds.

We must remain in charge. AI can assist, recommend, execute, and operate with
substantial independence, but people and institutions must retain both control
and responsibility. People are accountable. Machines are not.

# Open source makes responsibility visible

A commercial AI product is sold with representations about how it works, what
it can do, and how reliably or safely it will perform. The company providing
that product retains responsibility for those claims and for whether the
product functions as represented.

Open source is different. It traditionally comes without guarantees or a
warranty. People who choose to use, modify, train, or deploy it accept greater
responsibility for understanding what they are operating and determining
whether it is appropriate for their purpose.

That is not a weakness of open source. It is a more honest and empowering
relationship.

Open source gives people the ability to inspect the system, understand its
components, test its claims, correct problems, adapt it to their needs, and
share improvements. A closed system asks people to trust what its creator says.
An open system gives people and communities the opportunity to verify it.

Openness does not eliminate upstream responsibility. Maintainers and
contributors must still be truthful about what they provide, preserve
licensing and provenance, disclose known limitations, and answer for their own
decisions. But operators gain both the freedom and the responsibility to make
informed choices about what they put into use.

Open source does not make accountability disappear. It makes responsibility
more explicit and gives people the evidence and control needed to carry it.

# Freedom, responsibility, and receipts

This is one of the reasons we are building OpenWALDO.

OpenWALDO treats the sources of AI as a public, inspectable record. It records
the origins of training material, asserted licenses, immutable corpus objects,
model composes, tools, configurations, and the lineage connecting source
material to resulting artifacts.

An OpenWALDO AI Bill of Materials provides a record of what went into a model
and how that model was built. It does not declare that the model is safe. It
does not absolve the person using it. It does not determine legal guilt.

It provides receipts.

OpenWALDO is also a community. Builders, researchers, operators, companies,
hobbyists, students, and volunteers come together to share knowledge, improve
the corpus and tools, build models, examine evidence, and help one another take
AI to the next level. People who already understand AI can contribute their
experience, and people who want to learn can participate in the work and grow
alongside the project.

Open source gives people the freedom to inspect, understand, reproduce,
improve, and challenge the systems they use. That freedom is inseparable from
responsibility. People can make informed decisions only when they can see what
they are making decisions about.

As AI becomes more capable, we must not manufacture a new kind of unaccountable
actor. The machine may execute an action, but agency remains with the people
who trained it, authorized it, equipped it, and put it to work.

You can delegate the action.

You cannot delegate the responsibility.
