// the case

Open weights is not open source.

You wouldn’t call a compiled binary “open source” because you can download it. A model without its training data is exactly that: a binary without source. Here is why that matters — and what fixes it.

The industry argued itself into a corner

The labs selling closed access have spent years insisting that openly released models are dangerous — a story as old as GPT-2, withheld in 2019 as too unsafe to release in full, though the predicted harms never arrived. The same labs asked Congress to license who may build powerful models and signed statements ranking their own products alongside pandemics and nuclear war. It is an argument their business depends on: a model you can only rent through an API is a moat. And governments listened — model weights became export-controlled technology, and open release came to be treated as a proliferation risk rather than what it has always been in software: the mechanism of trust.

Then labs in China began releasing open-weight models that match — and sometimes beat — the closed frontier, freely and by the dozen. The scarcity story collapsed: frontier capability was never a guardable secret. The fear campaign hadn’t contained anything; it had only handicapped the West’s own open ecosystem and ceded open AI leadership to someone else. The response has been more of the same — calls to ban foreign models, statutory purges, renewed pushes with every new release — bans that are close to unenforceable against weights anyone can download.

Both sides are afraid of the same thing

All of it gets the threat model half right. The problem was never where a model comes from. The problem is that nobody can audit what went into it. An open-weight model without its training data is a binary without source: you can run it, but you cannot inspect it, verify it, or rebuild it. The fear of closed Western models and the fear of open Chinese ones are the same fear — being forced to trust weights no one can see into.

What’s missing isn’t policy. It’s an auditable source of data, and the toolchain to build trusted models from it.

Open source has won this argument before

Proprietary vendors made identical claims about open code — insecure, unaccountable, impossible to trust — and lost. Not because the world decided to trust strangers, but because source, licenses, and provenance made trust unnecessary: anyone could read the code, and anyone could rebuild it. Linux didn’t win by being certified safe; it won by being inspectable, forkable, and community-owned. Today it runs the world’s infrastructure — and the vendors who fought it build on it.

AI is missing exactly that property. And no export rule, no ban, and no safety pledge can substitute for it.

Closed data is the root of the fights we’re having

Look at what people are actually angry about in AI right now. Authors and artists discovering their work in training sets they never agreed to. Companies finding their content scraped without provenance or recourse. Labs accusing each other of distilling their models. Courts untangling what was pirated and what was licensed. Every one of these conflicts is a symptom of the same disease: the data is closed, so nobody can prove anything — not consent, not license, not origin, not absence.

Now imagine the opposite. Every byte in the corpus is openly licensed, recorded with its source and its SPDX tag, attested by a sign-off, removable on request, and pinned by hash into every model that trained on it. Consent is recorded. Attribution is carried. Takedown is a first-class operation. The argument doesn’t get won — it gets dissolved, the same way open source dissolved the argument about trusting code.

Trust through verification, not reputation

A model built on OpenWALDO carries its source. Every training run records a bill of materials — the exact index commit, the manifest hashes, the recipe, the tokenizer — into the model’s metadata, and forks preserve lineage. Anyone can audit it. Anyone can verify it. Anyone can recreate it. Where a model was trained stops mattering, because what it was trained on is on the record.

This is what “open source AI” has to mean if the words mean anything: not weights plus a press release, but the whole chain — Weights, Artifacts, Licenses, Data, Origins — open, verifiable, and rebuildable by anyone.

A commons, not a product

No single company can build this, and none should own it. What we need is a shared, collaborative base foundation — a corpus of human knowledge that anyone can obtain and anyone can contribute to — governed the way open source has governed itself for thirty years: maintainers, reviews, licenses, provenance, and a community that shows up. The corpus is the commons; the models are proof that it works.
