questions, answered

Frequently asked questions.

The short, practical answers. The long versions live in the project docs.

the basics
What exactly is OpenWALDO — a dataset or a tool?

Both, with the data at the center. The index (waldo-index) is the corpus: a git repository of curated, licensed, provenance-tracked training data. The toolchain (the waldo CLI) is one binary that manages the whole lifecycle — ingest, verify, train, fork, chat, export. What OpenWALDO is not is a model vendor: the models are what you build from the corpus. The project may publish small reference examples to prove the pipeline, but the product is the source, not the binary.

Why was OpenWALDO created?

Because open weights is not open source, and the fights consuming AI — distillation accusations, creators trained on without consent, models nobody can see into — are all downstream of closed data. OpenWALDO was founded by Gregory Kurtzer (CentOS, Warewulf, Singularity/Apptainer, Rocky Linux) to do what he has done his whole career: build the open, shared foundation and grow the community that makes it permanent — this time for the data AI is built from. The full story is on the About page.

Why does open source matter?

Because it’s the only mechanism humanity has found for building technology that strangers can trust without permission and without taking anyone’s word for it. Source, licenses, and provenance replace reputation with verification: anyone can read it, anyone can rebuild it, anyone can fix it, and no vendor can take it away. That’s not ideology — it’s how collaboration outcompeted secrecy in software, decade after decade. AI is the newest layer of critical infrastructure, and it’s the only one trying to skip that step.

What has open source accomplished — and who depends on it?

Practically everything, and practically everyone. Linux runs every one of the world’s top 500 supercomputers, every major cloud, and — as Android — most of the phones on Earth. Apache and Nginx serve most of the web; OpenSSL encrypts it; the internet itself runs on open implementations of open protocols. Git is how software is written, Kubernetes is how it’s run, Python is the language AI is built in, and GCC and LLVM compile all of it. Audits routinely find open source components in over ninety percent of commercial codebases — every bank, government, hospital, and car depends on it, whether they know it or not. The pattern is a century’s worth of evidence compressed into thirty years: the shared foundation wins.

Why aren’t today’s “open source” models actually open source?

Bluntly: because you can’t see the source. Weights are the compiled output of training — releasing them is releasing a binary. Many open-weight models do carry genuinely open licenses (Apache-2.0, MIT) — and the license isn’t the problem. A license can only open what’s included, and what’s never included is the training data, the curation, and the provenance that produced the weights. However permissive the terms, that’s freeware with a press release. Open source has a definition, and it has always meant the source: you can inspect it, verify it, rebuild it, and change it. Until a model ships with its data — Weights, Artifacts, Licenses, Data, Origins, all five — the words don’t apply.

How is this different from other “open” AI projects?

Most “open” releases are open weights: you can download the model, but not see, verify, or rebuild what went into it — a binary without source. A few excellent projects (OLMo, the Common Pile) publish their data too, and OpenWALDO builds on their work. What OpenWALDO adds is the commons infrastructure: a contribution model (git + DCO + review), per-shard licensing, federated hosting, content-addressed verification, and a model lifecycle that records the data-to-weights chain automatically — so the corpus grows permissionlessly, like an open source project, instead of being one lab’s dataset drop.

Why not just use the Common Pile wholesale?

We do use it — with credit where it’s due: EleutherAI’s Common Pile proved that license-clean pretraining works at all, and OpenWALDO is ingesting it. But ingesting a dataset is very different from pointing at one, because a dataset is not an index.

Mixed licenses in one bundle. Across the corpus, the Common Pile blends public domain, CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, and permissive content — each document under its own license. At ingest, OpenWALDO partitions everything into single-license shards with per-shard SPDX tags — so a consumer can take exactly the license profile they want and prove what they took.

No chain of responsibility. A dataset drop has no DCO sign-offs — no named person attesting, contribution by contribution, that each piece is redistributable as labeled. In the index, every shard traces to a signed commit by a responsible party, and every source is recorded with its origin and hash.

A snapshot, not a commons. A dataset is frozen at release; the index grows by reviewed pull request, records provenance one hop closer to the source wherever we can fetch upstream directly (Gutenberg from Gutenberg, Wikipedia from its dumps), makes overlap visible through content hashes, and supports removal by commit.

What about the other big open datasets — Dolma, FineWeb, Common Corpus, ROOTS?

Each one fails wholesale adoption for a different reason — and the reasons, taken together, are the argument for an audited index. Compilation isn’t content: Dolma and FineWeb carry real open licenses on the bundle, but the web-crawled text underneath is ordinary all-rights-reserved copyright, so only their genuinely open slices qualify. Gating: ROOTS sits behind an access agreement, and OpenWALDO shards must be anonymously fetchable by anyone. Metadata gaps: Common Corpus has honest open ambitions, but parts lack per-document license records — and license metadata can’t be trusted blindly. Even EleutherAI, curating the Common Pile, concluded that “identifying the license a particular work has is surprisingly hard and automatic tools … are not reliable enough to meet our standards,” which is why per-document provenance, not a bundle license, is the bar. The sourcing rule that falls out: fetch from the most upstream source we can reach ourselves, and accept a curated compilation only when the compilation itself is openly licensed, versioned, and recorded as the source. The goal is an open and auditable index — every byte answerable to a license, a source, and a person.

Do I need an account or permission to use any of this?

No. The index is a public git repository, and every shard of data is anonymously fetchable from its committed URL — reads never require credentials. Clone, fetch, verify, train. You need credentials only to host shards on your own storage, and a GitHub account only to open pull requests.

for AI labs & model builders
Does OpenWALDO fit into how an AI Lab already does training?

Yes, probably, you can use it as part of your base layers and spend your effort on the data that makes your models different. Every lab independently re-collects the same foundational corpus: the books, the wikis, the code, the papers, the law. That work is undifferentiated, expensive, and legally fraught — and it’s exactly what a commons is for. Pull OpenWALDO as the substrate for a from-scratch base model, or augment your existing training mix with its license-clean corpora, and put your researchers on the curation, synthesis, and post-training that actually distinguish your models. OpenWALDO isn’t competing with your models; it’s the part of every model that never should have been a competition.

And you get something no private data pipeline can give you: a provable answer to “what is this model based on?” Training on indexed data pins a bill of materials — the exact index commit, manifest hashes, and per-shard licenses — so your customers, your counsel, and your regulators can verify the claim instead of taking your word for it. When part of the diet is OpenWALDO, that part of the audit is already done, in public. Ship the OpenWALDO badge with your model and the claim is one click from its proof.

What can I do with the corpus?

Anything its licenses allow — and because every shard carries its own SPDX license tag, you can filter to exactly the license profile your use requires. In practice: pretrain models from scratch, fine-tune or continue-pretrain existing models, build domain-specific models, run dataset research and ablations, teach with it, or mirror it. The corpus is deliberately ordinary data (zstd-compressed JSONL) so nothing about it locks you into the waldo toolchain.

Can I use OpenWALDO with existing models and training stacks?

Yes. Shards are plain zstd-compressed JSONL at stable URLs, verified by sha256 — any training pipeline that can read JSONL can consume them, with or without the waldo CLI. And the toolchain itself supports starting from existing weights: waldo model pull imports a model, and training it on indexed paths records a BOM for everything from that point forward. The full audit-all-the-way-down story applies to from-scratch models, but the corpus and the lineage records work with the models you already have.

What hardware do I need to train something?

A laptop, to start — with expectations set honestly. The smallest rungs of the growth ladder (10–100M parameters) train on Apple Silicon via MLX or any single GPU, and the ten-minute walkthrough produces a real, provenanced model on a MacBook. But a model that size generates incoherent baby babble — English-shaped, grammatical even, and meaningless. That rung exists to prove the pipeline and the provenance chain, not to talk to. Useful models take bigger machines and the same commands: PyTorch for single/multi-GPU Linux boxes, torchtitan for multi-node. The tooling scales; your ambition and compute budget set the rung.

What’s in the corpus, license-wise?

Only content that is openly licensed and freely redistributable — nothing gated, nothing noncommercial, nothing no-derivatives. That means public-domain works, openly licensed text (attribution and share-alike styles), and open source code — both permissive and copyleft/GNU-licensed. Copyleft content is included deliberately (it roughly doubles the clean pool), and because every shard carries its license tag, it’s trivially filterable: if you want to ensure no copyleft reaches your training mix, exclude those tags and train on exactly the license profile you choose.

Can I use OpenWALDO models and data commercially?

That’s the intent — the official tree deliberately excludes noncommercial and no-derivative licenses. Obligations that do exist (attribution for CC-BY, share-alike tags, code NOTICE files) are recorded per shard, so you — and your counsel — can see exactly what applies and train on precisely the license profile your product allows. The index makes compliance auditable; what your situation requires is between you and your lawyers, as with any open source license question.

for companies & organizations
As a company, what should we add to the corpus so AI models know about us?

Anything you want the world’s models to answer correctly about — and are willing to openly license: product documentation, manuals, API references, tutorials, knowledge bases, spec sheets, release notes, published research, industry expertise. Plain text, Markdown, JSONL, HTML, or EPUB — the converter senses each file’s format. One ingest and one DCO-signed pull request later, every model trained on the tree knows your products, with provenance instead of hallucination — and the manifests record your company as the source, permanently. The contribution flow walks through it end to end.

Can I run it privately — internal data, air-gapped, my own storage?

Yes. The toolchain has no dependency on the project’s infrastructure: start a private tree with waldo index init, point the lookaside at your own S3 bucket or local storage, and ingest and train on data that never leaves your network. You get the same determinism, license tracking, and BOMs — which is exactly what internal compliance wants — without publishing anything.

for contributors & curators
How do I contribute data?

Git-style, in four steps: gather openly licensed data into a directory (however you like — the fetchers/ are worked examples), run waldo index ingest to convert, pack, and push the shards to your storage, then git commit -s and open a pull request. The commit’s DCO sign-off is your attestation that the data is redistributable under the license you declared; the diff plus the URLs in it are the entire contribution. CI re-verifies the whole tree on your PR.

What data qualifies for the official tree?

Content that is genuinely open at the document level: public domain, CC-BY, CC-BY-SA, permissively licensed code (per-file), and synthetic data from generators whose licenses place no restrictions on outputs. The bar applies to the underlying documents, not a dataset’s compilation license — which is why CommonCrawl-derived corpora, gated “research-only” collections, and GPT-distilled synthetic sets don’t qualify, however good they are. The gaps that bar creates (math is the biggest) are the project’s top contribution priorities.

I know a great dataset. How do I request that it be reviewed and included?

Propose it in the open: open an issue on the waldo-index repo — or raise it on Slack — describing what the data is, where it lives upstream, and the evidence that the underlying documents are openly licensed (the license bar judges documents, not a bundle’s compilation license). The strongest proposal is a pull request with the fetcher and ingest done, but a well-documented pointer is a real contribution too: a reviewer or corpus deputy can pick it up from there.

I don’t have data. How else can I help?

Four other ways: code (the Go toolchain — converters, fetchers, training backends), hosting (shard storage and mirrors are federated and contributor-run), review (checking license claims and provenance on data PRs is as valuable as the data itself), and compute (the larger rungs of the growth ladder run on donated or funded cluster time). Docs, examples, and evangelism all count too. And the simplest way to start is just to show up: join the Slack, say hello, and find your place to plug in.

Who hosts the data, and what does it cost the project?

Contributors host their own shards — any S3 bucket or static HTTPS host works, and content addressing means every copy is verifiable and mirrorable. The project itself holds only the small git-tracked metadata. That’s the design: no central storage bill, no single point of failure, and no gatekeeper who can decide the corpus disappears.

trust, auditing & rights
How do I audit a model built on OpenWALDO?

To be precise: models don’t come from OpenWALDO — they’re built on it, by whoever trains them. When the waldo tooling does the training, it embeds a bill of materials automatically: the exact index commit, the manifest hashes consumed, the recipe, tokenizer, and backend of every run — written into model.json and shipped human-readable as PROVENANCE.md, with lineage chained across forks. Builders using their own training stacks are strongly encouraged to embed or distribute the same BOM with their models. To audit one: read the BOM, check out the index at the pinned commit, and run waldo index verify --fetch to confirm every referenced shard exists and matches its hash. At that point you can inspect any byte the model trained on — or rebuild it yourself from the same inputs.

Can a model be reproduced exactly?

The inputs replay exactly: anyone with the BOM gets byte-identical data, the same recipe, and the same architecture. The resulting weights are equivalent but not bit-identical across different hardware, because GPU training isn’t bit-deterministic — the same honest caveat every reproducibility effort in the field carries. What matters for trust is that the claim “this model was built from this data” is checkable, and it is.

How do I find out whether my content is in the corpus?

Look — everything is public and inspectable. Every manifest in the index records its sources by name, upstream origin, version, and hash, so searching the tree for your site, name, or project answers most questions in seconds. And because every shard is anonymously downloadable, you can inspect the actual records, not just the metadata. If you find your content and want it gone, see the next question — removal is a first-class operation.

What if my content ends up in the corpus and I want it out?

Takedown is a first-class operation, not a support ticket. Removal happens in the open — the shard leaves the index by commit, storage operators garbage-collect it, and because every model records what it trained on, the record of what was used when stays honest. Openness includes the right to leave.
